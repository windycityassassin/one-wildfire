You'll see 12 clusters of satellite fire detections, coloured from first to last day. For each one, decide whether it was made by one fire or by several separate fires. Some are from US fires where the answer is on record; some are from Indonesia and the Kansas prairie, where no one has mapped it.

Each cluster is shown in one of four views, rotated so every view gets used: a flat map, pre-fire satellite imagery, 3D terrain, or a space-time cube where height is time. Building on Cattau et al. 2016

How many fires does the satellite get right?

Satellites don't see fires, just hot pixels. The 2016 paper turned those pixels into fires with one simple rule. I rebuilt it and tested it on 1,431 US fires to see how far it goes today.

  1. 57%
    The 2016 rule, as is It was made for peat fires in Indonesia, so this is just my starting line.
  2. 75%
    My best automatic try Machine learning included. Nothing I tried got past this.
  3. 88%
    Plus a person In my simulation, someone checks just the biggest 5% of fires.

What I found

The wall at 75% is fires that burn into each other. From space they look like one fire. A computer can't tell them apart, but a person often can, as long as you show them the right ones.

Data: NASA FIRMS and US interagency fire maps, 2020–2024. I built this with an AI assistant doing most of the coding.

How I tested it

I ran the 2016 rule on five years of US fires and checked every answer against the official fire maps. Then I tried to beat it.

Same story in every region

Which fires a person should check

What I took from the 2016 paper

The rule: satellite detections in the same or next-door 1 km square within 4 days are one fire, and the earliest one is where it started. The paper used it to find where fires begin in Indonesia's peat swamps. A 2025 follow-up (Mahood et al.) notes the right window changes from place to place, and most countries have no fire maps to check it against. The US does, so that's where I tested it.

How I scored it

  • 565,000 NASA satellite detections and 1,431 official fires, 2020–2024.
  • A fire counts as right when one group holds at least 90% of its detections and that group is at least 90% that fire.
  • I split the US into six regions and always scored on a region I didn't tune on.

What's still rough

  • The 88% is a simulation. It assumes the person always splits a merged fire correctly.
  • I only used MODIS (1 km pixels). The newer VIIRS sensor (375 m) could change the picture.
  • "One fire" in official records can be a whole complex of lightning starts.

What I'd do next

  • Try finer satellite data to see if it breaks the 75% wall.
  • Build the review step for real: the system suggests where to split a big fire, and a fire manager or local resident confirms it.
